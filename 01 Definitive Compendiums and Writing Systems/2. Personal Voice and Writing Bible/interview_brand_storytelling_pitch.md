# STEPHEN AGBEDE — INTERVIEW BRAND STORYTELLING PITCH

**Purpose:** Modular talk tracks for pitching your experience through brand storytelling in any interview — marketing roles, strategy roles, growth leadership, agency pitches.

> [!TIP]
> These are not scripts to memorize word-for-word. They are **story shapes** — frameworks with your real details pre-loaded. Internalize the shape, then speak it in your own rhythm. Every story here comes from your actual work. Nothing is invented.

---

## THE OPENING — "How I Work" (60–90 seconds)

**Use when they say:** *"Tell me about yourself"* or *"Walk me through your experience."*

> Most marketers start with a channel. *"I'm a social media guy,"* or *"I run paid ads."* I start with a question: **what is the one thing this market is actually frustrated about that nobody has said out loud yet?**
>
> That's the thread I pull. Once I find it, everything else — the copy, the funnel, the ads, the content calendar — gets built around that single frustration. Not around the product features. Not around what the founder thinks is impressive. Around what makes the buyer lean forward and say *"finally, someone said it."*
>
> I've done that across fintech, Web3, physical consumer products, events, women-in-tech communities, and school management SaaS. Seven years, always a different industry, always the same first step: find the bleeding neck before you pick up a bandage.

## STORY 1 — "THE OFFER THAT MOVED A MILLION DOLLARS"
**MogulPress | $1M+ in client sales**

**Use when they ask about:** revenue impact, high-ticket sales, offer strategy, or direct-response copywriting.

> I joined MogulPress when they had a service people respected but no offer anyone felt compelled to buy *today.*
>
> PR retainers are a slow sell. People know they want press coverage, but they don't feel urgency about it the way they feel urgency about, say, a broken landing page or a sales slump. So the question was: how do you make a "nice-to-have" feel like a "need-it-now"?
>
> I rebuilt the entire positioning around the client's real fear — not "I need press," but *"I've built something real and nobody outside my circle knows I exist."* That's the bleeding neck. Invisibility. Then I engineered the offer framework — risk reversal, specificity, a structure that made saying yes feel safer than saying "let me think about it."
>
> Over the engagement, that single offer architecture generated over a million dollars in gross client sales. Not from more traffic. From the same pipeline, converting harder, because the story finally matched what the buyer was already feeling.

## STORY 2 — "FIVE-CENT INSTALLS IN A MARKET THAT PAYS FIVE DOLLARS"
**1app Fintech | 20,000+ downloads, 25% CAC reduction**

**Use when they ask about:** performance marketing, paid acquisition, scaling on a budget, or working with data.

> 1app had the product. Solid fintech app, real value. But customer acquisition was eating the budget. Every install cost more than it should have, because the campaigns were broad — targeting everyone who might theoretically want a payments app.
>
> I tightened everything. Ruthless audience exclusions first — stop paying to show ads to people who've already installed, people outside your service corridor, people whose behaviour says "just browsing." Then I split-tested the creative, not just A versus B, but isolating which *specific frustration* in the ad copy drove the click. Was it speed? Was it fees? Was it trust?
>
> Turns out it was trust. The market was tired of fintech apps that looked slick but felt risky. So the winning creative leaned into safety and reliability, not flash.
>
> We got the cost per install down to five cents in a market where others were paying five dollars. Over twenty thousand verified app downloads. And a 25% reduction in customer acquisition cost overall, which meant every naira spent was pulling harder.

## STORY 3 — "THE ROOM NOBODY WAS INVITED TO"
**BCP Origins | TBoT 2026 Conference Strategy**

**Use when they ask about:** brand strategy, event marketing, building desirability, or working with limited budgets.

> BCP Origins had done big events before — three thousand people in one room. But for 2026, the founder wanted something different. Not bigger. *Smaller.* An exclusive dinner in Lagos for a handful of key stakeholders.
>
> The challenge was: how do you generate the energy of a three-thousand-person conference with a thirty-person guest list?
>
> I built the entire campaign around a single tension: **visibility without access.** Everyone would *know* the event was happening. Almost no one would be *in* it. We called it "The Beginning of Tomorrow." Every piece of content, across every channel, answered one question — *"What is the beginning of tomorrow?"* — and if a piece of content didn't connect back to that question, it didn't ship.
>
> No speaker names before the event. No guest list. No registration link until the final week. Just the question, repeated, refracted, carried by different voices — the founder, past attendees, the team — each adding a layer, never the full answer.
>
> The post-event campaign carried equal creative weight to the eight weeks before it. Because the real payoff wasn't filling seats. It was making every relationship formed in that room compound for the next twelve months.

## STORY 4 — "SEVEN SCHOOLS AND ONE ALUMNI ASSOCIATION"
**EportalNet / Hunnovate | School Management SaaS GTM**

**Use when they ask about:** go-to-market strategy, field sales, B2B, or adapting strategy based on real-world feedback.

> EportalNet was a school management platform — solid product, real problem. But no go-to-market strategy. I wrote the full GTM document before we knocked on a single door: market sizing, ICP, competitive mapping, pricing tiers aligned to the Nigerian school-term cycle, a ninety-day execution plan.
>
> Then we went into the field. Canvassed seven schools in Akure. And here's where the story gets interesting — one of those visits was to a public school, Oyemekun Grammar, which we'd expected to be a dead end because public schools don't control their own software budgets.
>
> But Oyemekun had a strong alumni association and an active PTA. And those alumni *did* influence infrastructure decisions. So we discovered a second entry route nobody had planned for: pitch the platform as an alumni-driven school development initiative, not a vendor product.
>
> That single field insight reshaped the entire outreach strategy. The document I wrote before we started was important — it gave us the framework. But the framework was only as good as our willingness to let the field talk back to it.

## STORY 5 — "FORTY-TWO PERCENT OPEN RATES IN A WORLD THAT AVERAGES EIGHTEEN"
**Reply.cash | Web3 Community & Growth**

**Use when they ask about:** retention, community, email marketing, or Web3/crypto experience.

> Reply.cash is a stablecoin remittance product on Solana and Arbitrum. The user base is African — Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, plus eighteen other corridors. My job was growth, but the real problem was retention.
>
> In Web3, people join communities the way they try new restaurants — curious today, gone tomorrow. So I focused on making the community a place people returned to because it was *useful*, not because we were pinging them with announcements.
>
> I built over seven hundred intent-based use cases across African remittance corridors — real scenarios people actually faced when sending money home. "My aunt in Kumasi needs school fees by Friday" — that kind of specificity. The content wasn't about the product. It was about the *moment* the product becomes necessary.
>
> The community grew to nineteen thousand active members. Newsletter open rates held steady above forty-two percent — in an industry where eighteen percent is considered good. Because people opened the emails for the same reason they stayed in the community: it spoke to something they were actually dealing with, not something we wished they cared about.

## STORY 6 — "THE ANNUAL REPORT THAT WASN'T A REPORT"
**4Herfrika | Year 2 Annual Report & Becoming Her Launch**

**Use when they ask about:** nonprofit/impact work, storytelling, writing ability, or brand voice.

> 4Herfrika is a women's empowerment community running across multiple university campuses in Nigeria. The founder needed a Year 2 annual report, but what she actually needed was a story that made donors, partners, and members feel the momentum — not just read the numbers.
>
> So I didn't write a report. I wrote a narrative shaped around one metaphor — "two steps forward" — and held that thread from the opening line to the last sentence. Every section — the registration milestone, the campus outreach, the mentorship pilot, the challenges — was told through that single lens. Two steps. Not a sprint. Not a revolution. Two honest steps.
>
> The mentorship programme, Becoming Her, got its own launch email. The subject line was five words: *"Now you know who to call."* Because the whole programme was born from one recurring question members kept asking in DMs — *"What happens after this? Who do I call now?"* The email didn't sell a feature. It answered a question people had already been carrying.
>
> That's brand storytelling in its purest form. You don't invent the story. You listen until you hear it, and then you say it back clearer than they could say it themselves.

## STORY 7 — "ASK BEFORE YOU BUILD"
**ZendPay | Brand Audit & Strategy Discipline**

**Use when they ask about:** process, how you handle ambiguity, or situations where you pushed back on a client.

> ZendPay came to me with a brand document, a content plan calling for eighty-four posts a month, and a live product that contradicted both.
>
> The brand guide said never show faces, never use stock photography. The live website was entirely lifestyle photography with faces. The content plan quoted specific numbers — fifty million naira processed, 98.7% voice accuracy — but the site said "open beta." The brand doc banned crypto language, but the product's receive flow showed a visible Solana address.
>
> I could have just started writing posts. That's what most people would have done — take the brief at face value, start producing. Instead, I sent back a list of clarifying questions. *Which name is canonical — Zend or ZendPay? Are those numbers real or placeholders? Is the brand doc adopted policy or a draft nobody reviewed? What's the one metric that matters in ninety days?*
>
> I deliberately paused all strategy work until I had answers. Because writing content on top of an unresolved contradiction doesn't produce marketing. It produces noise. And noise is more expensive to clean up later than silence is now.

## THE CLOSER — "What I Actually Do" (30 seconds)

**Use at the end, or when they ask:** *"So what would you say you bring to a team?"*

> I find the one sentence the market is already feeling but hasn't heard anyone say yet. Then I build everything — the offer, the funnel, the content, the community — around that sentence. I've done it for a million-dollar PR agency and a campus women's group that distributes sanitary pads in Makoko. The industries change. The method doesn't. Find the bleeding neck, say it out loud, and build a system that keeps the pressure on it.

## HOW TO USE THIS DOCUMENT

1. **Before any interview**, re-read the opening and the closer. Those are your anchors — they frame everything else.
2. **Pick 2–3 stories** that match the role you're interviewing for. Don't try to tell all seven.
3. **Lead with the frustration, not the feature.** Every story above starts with a problem the market was feeling. That's the hook. The solution is the payoff.
4. **Keep each story under 90 seconds** when spoken. These written versions run longer for detail — when you speak them, compress to: *problem → what I did differently → specific result.*
5. **Never open with credentials.** Your rule. Expertise shows through specificity, not announcements.
